Here's a question I keep coming back to.

If most cybersecurity failures are human and boring (a reused password, an old account nobody switched off, someone clicking the link they *knew* they shouldn't), why do so many organizations spend their security budget on expensive, impressive-looking software?

I don't think the answer is that vendors are overcomplicating security to sell us products. Although it certainly doesn't hurt sales. I think the answer is simpler. Software is easy to buy. You sign a purchase order, someone installs it, and there's a dashboard to show management. Discipline is much harder to buy, because it looks like nothing. Nobody gets a dashboard for "we removed fourteen accounts belonging to people who left last year."

But discipline is what actually stops most attacks.

## It almost always comes down to access

Strip away the jargon and almost every serious incident comes down to one thing: **someone gained access they shouldn't have had.** Usually not through brilliant hacking. Usually through:

- weak or reused passwords
- no multi-factor authentication
- old staff accounts that are still active
- shared login details
- too many people with admin rights
- nobody regularly reviewing who has access to what

Notice what isn't on that list: "didn't have antivirus." Plenty of organizations believe antivirus is enough. It's useful, but it does nothing to stop someone who logs in with a valid username and password. As far as your systems are concerned, that person *is* you.

## Sometimes the enemy is you

They say keep your enemies close. Sometimes the enemy is already inside the building, and it's us.

You are the threat when you open the attachment you knew you shouldn't download. When you enter that competition to win a free iPhone (I mean, come on). When you skip multi-factor authentication because it's annoying. When your password is on a sticky note next to your keyboard.

This isn't a lecture. That's normal human behavior. Which is exactly the point: organizations need processes that *assume* people will make mistakes, rather than hoping they won't.

## Three stories, one pattern

**The insider and the SIM card.** In 2022, police in Malawi arrested four suspects in a SIM-swap scheme used to empty customers' bank accounts. Among them were a mobile network branch employee and a mobile money agent. Nobody had to break through a bank's firewall. The fraud ran through a trusted business process and people who had legitimate access to it.

**The password that was "password".** In 2022, a group that breached TransUnion South Africa claimed the account they used was protected by the password "password". TransUnion said a client's credentials had been misused. The group also claimed it had been inside TransUnion's systems since 2012 without being noticed. That claim hasn't been proven, but it points to the scarier lesson. Attackers don't always smash and grab. They get in, stay quiet, and keep their access. That's why *detection*, actively hunting for things that shouldn't be there, matters as much as prevention.

**No hacking required.** In 2020, Experian South Africa disclosed that a fraudster posing as a legitimate client had been handed personal information on about 24 million people and nearly 800,000 businesses. No one broke in. Someone just asked convincingly, and nobody checked properly.

None of these needed a genius. Each needed only one weak password, one weak process, or one person who didn't verify.

## It's not about complexity. It's about discipline.

Here's what actually moves the needle for most organizations:

- unique passwords for every account
- multi-factor authentication everywhere it's possible
- regular reviews of who has access to what
- immediate removal of accounts that are no longer needed
- secure backups that are actually tested
- clear internal policies, including verifying requests before releasing data, money, or SIM cards

None of this is glamorous. It's like leaving for work on time. Nobody congratulates you for it. But leave late a few times and you'll see what happens: traffic, a missed meeting, a whole day that went wrong because of one small skipped habit.

Security works the same way. If you're not disciplined, expect disaster.

## The Seculens takeaway

**Attackers rarely break in. They log in.** Tools help, but discipline decides: who has access, how they prove it's really them, and how quickly you take it away when they no longer need it.
